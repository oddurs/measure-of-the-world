\chapter{Primary Source Documents}
\label{app:primary-sources}

This appendix presents excerpts from primary sources cited throughout the book. Each is quoted from the original publication, with the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century orthography preserved: the long \emph{s} of the period is rendered here as \emph{f} in the printed sources, and appears below as it does on the page. Full citations are given with each excerpt and in Appendix~\ref{app:bibliography}.

\section{Excerpt 1: Flamsteed's \emph{Historia Coelestis Britannica}}

Flamsteed's great catalogue was published posthumously in three volumes in 1725, in Latin throughout. Its preface, the \emph{Prolegomena} to volume~3, sets out the programme of observation that occupied him from 1675 until his death in 1719. An English translation was published by the National Maritime Museum in 1982.\footnote{John Flamsteed, \emph{Historiae Coelestis Britannicae Volumen}, 3 vols. (London, 1725). The translated preface is Allan Chapman (ed.), \emph{The Preface to John Flamsteed's Historia Coelestis Britannica}, trans. Alison Dione Johnson, Maritime Monographs and Reports No.~52 (Greenwich: National Maritime Museum, 1982).}

The catalogue records nearly three thousand stars, observed over more than four decades with the mural arc and the transit instrument described in Chapter~\ref{ch:mural-arc-transits}. Its purpose was practical as much as scientific: an accurate catalogue of fixed stars was the reference frame against which the Moon's position had to be measured, and lunar position was the route to longitude at sea.

\section{Excerpt 2: Bradley on the Aberration of Light}

From Bradley's letter to Edmond Halley, read to the Royal Society and published in \emph{Philosophical Transactions} in 1729.\footnote{James Bradley, ``A Letter from the Reverend Mr.~James Bradley Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, and F.R.S. to Dr.~Edmond Halley Astronom.\ Reg.\ \&c.\ giving an Account of a new discovered Motion of the Fix'd Stars'', \emph{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London} 35, no.~406 (1729): 637--661.}

\begin{quote}
``SIR, YOU having been pleafed to exprefs your Satisfaction with what I had an Opportunity fometime ago, of telling you in Converfation, concerning fome Obfervations, that were making by our late worthy and ingenious Friend, the honourable Samuel Molyneux Efquire, and which have fince been continued and repeated by my felf, in order to determine the Parallax of the fixt Stars; I fhall now beg leave to lay before you a more particular Account of them.''
\end{quote}

Bradley then describes the beginning of the series:

\begin{quote}
``Mr.~Molyneux's Apparatus was compleated and fitted for obferving about the End of November 1725, and on the third Day of December following, the bright Star in the Head of Draco (marked $\gamma$ by Bayer) was for the firft Time obferved, as it paffed near the Zenith, and its Situation carefully taken with the Inftrument.''
\end{quote}

And the explanation, when it came:

\begin{quote}
``At laft I conjectured, that all the Phaenomena hitherto mentioned, proceeded from the progreffive Motion of Light and the Earth's annual Motion in its Orbit. For I perceived, that, if Light was propagated in Time, the apparent Place of a fixt Object would not be the fame when the Eye is at Reft, as when it is moving in any other Direction, than that of the Line paffing through the Eye and Object.''
\end{quote}

\noindent\textsc{Context}: Two details of this account are easily lost. The instrument was Molyneux's, erected at his house at Kew, and Bradley was using it as a collaborator rather than as its owner; his own sector at Wanstead came later. And the observations began as a search for parallax, which they failed to find. Aberration was what the data turned out to contain instead. The geometry is derived in Chapter~\ref{ch:bradley-aberration} and Appendix~\ref{app:mathematical-derivations}.

\section{Excerpt 3: The First \emph{Nautical Almanac}}

The preface to the first \emph{Nautical Almanac}, for the year 1767. The volume carries an imprint date of 1766; copies did not reach the Commissioners until 6~January 1767. The preface is written in the Commissioners' voice, not Maskelyne's own.\footnote{\emph{The Nautical Almanac and Astronomical Ephemeris, for the Year 1767} (London: Commissioners of Longitude, 1766), preface.}

\begin{quote}
``THE Commiffioners of Longitude, in purfuance of the Powers vefted in them by a late Act of Parliament, prefent the Publick with the NAUTICAL ALMANAC and ASTRONOMICAL EPHEMERIS for the Year 1767, to be continued annually; a Work which muft greatly contribute to the Improvement of Aftronomy, Geography, and Navigation. This Ephemeris contains every Thing effential to general Ufe that is to be found in any Ephemeris hitherto publifhed, with many other ufeful and interefting Particulars never yet offered to the Publick in any Work of this Kind. The Tables of the Moon had been brought by the late Profeffor Mayer of Gottingen to a fufficient Exactnefs to determine the Longitude at Sea, within a Degree, as appeared by the Trials of feveral Perfons who made Ufe of them.''
\end{quote}

\noindent\textsc{Context}: Note what is claimed and what is not. A degree of longitude is about sixty nautical miles at the equator, and the Commissioners say so plainly. This is the standard the lunar distance method was expected to meet, and it is the figure against which Harrison's chronometers should be judged (Chapters~\ref{ch:lunar-distance} and~\ref{ch:harrison-chronometers}). The passage also credits Tobias Mayer of G\"ottingen, whose lunar tables made the method practical and whose widow received a Board of Longitude award for them.

\section{Excerpt 4: The 1884 International Meridian Conference}

The seven resolutions of the Final Act, adopted in Washington in October 1884.\footnote{\emph{International Conference Held at Washington for the Purpose of Fixing a Prime Meridian and a Universal Day, October 1884: Protocols of the Proceedings} (Washington: Gibson Bros., 1884).}

\begin{quote}
I. ``That it is the opinion of this Congress that it is desirable to adopt a single prime meridian for all nations, in place of the multiplicity of initial meridians which now exist.''

II. ``That the Conference proposes to the Governments here represented the adoption of the meridian passing through the centre of the transit instrument at the Observatory of Greenwich as the initial meridian for longitude.''

III. ``That from this meridian longitude shall be counted in two directions up to 180 degrees, east longitude being plus and west longitude minus.''

IV. ``That the Conference proposes the adoption of a universal day for all purposes for which it may be found convenient, and which shall not interfere with the use of local or other standard time where desirable.''

V. ``That this universal day is to be a mean solar day; is to begin for all the world at the moment of mean midnight of the initial meridian, coinciding with the beginning of the civil day and date of that meridian; and is to be counted from zero up to twenty-four hours.''

VI. ``That the Conference expresses the hope that as soon as may be practicable the astronomical and nautical days will be arranged everywhere to begin at mean midnight.''

VII. ``That the Conference expresses the hope that the technical studies designed to regulate and extend the application of the decimal system to the division of angular space and of time shall be resumed, so as to permit the extension of this application to all cases in which it presents real advantages.''
\end{quote}

\noindent\textsc{Context}: The conference is often credited with creating the world's time zones. It did not. No resolution mentions them: time zones were adopted piecemeal by individual states over the following decades, and the Conference's contribution was the universal day of Resolution~V, counted from midnight at a single agreed meridian, which is the thing time zones are offsets from. Resolution~II is also more specific than it is usually reported to be. The meridian adopted is not Greenwich in general but the centre of one instrument, the Airy Transit Circle, which is why the modern geodetic meridian lies about 102~metres to its east (Chapter~\ref{ch:meridian-conference}).

\section{Excerpt 5: The 1919 Eclipse Result}

From the general conclusions of Dyson, Eddington and Davidson, reporting the two expeditions sent to observe the total solar eclipse of 29~May 1919.\footnote{F.~W. Dyson, A.~S. Eddington and C.~Davidson, ``IX. A Determination of the Deflection of Light by the Sun's Gravitational Field, from Observations Made at the Total Eclipse of May~29, 1919'', \emph{Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London, Series A} 220 (1920): 291--333.}

\begin{quote}
``In summarising the results of the two expeditions, the greatest weight must be attached to those obtained with the 4-inch lens at Sobral. \ldots{} The result from declinations is about twice the weight of that from right ascensions, so that the mean result is $1''\!\cdot\!98$ with a probable error of about $\pm 0''\!\cdot\!12$.

The Principe observations were generally interfered with by cloud. \ldots{} The deflection obtained was $1''\!\cdot\!61$. The probable error is about $\pm 0''\!\cdot\!305$, so that the result has much less weight than the preceding.

Both of these point to the full deflection $1''\!\cdot\!75$ of Einstein's generalised relativity theory, the Sobral results definitely, and the Principe results perhaps with some uncertainty. There remain the Sobral astrographic plates which gave the deflection $0''\!\cdot\!93$, discordant by an amount much beyond the limits of its accidental error.''
\end{quote}

\noindent\textsc{Context}: This is the most misquoted result in twentieth-century astronomy, and the passage shows why. There are three measurements, not one: $1''\!\cdot\!98$, $1''\!\cdot\!61$ and $0''\!\cdot\!93$. Einstein's prediction of $1''\!\cdot\!75$ is a prediction, not a measurement, and popular accounts routinely report it as though the expeditions had measured it. The third value is closer to the Newtonian half-deflection than to Einstein's, and the authors say so without concealment before setting it aside on the grounds of a focus change at Sobral. Whether that exclusion was justified has been argued ever since. The expedition was organised from Greenwich by Frank Watson Dyson, then Astronomer Royal, and it is the Observatory's most famous single result. The narrative chapters do not cover it; only the chronology in Appendix~\ref{app:chronologies} records it. That is a gap in the book, not in the source.

\section*{A Note on This Appendix}

Every passage above is quoted from the publication cited in its footnote, with spelling and typography as printed. Where a source could not be reached in facsimile, no excerpt is offered. An excerpt whose wording cannot be checked against the original does not belong in a book about the honest representation of measurement.
