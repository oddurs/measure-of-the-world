# Appendix A: derivation check

Generated by running `scripts/verify/derivations.py` on 9 September 2026 and
reading the results against the manuscript. 18 of 25 recomputed values agree
with what the book prints. The seven that do not are below.

Six of them are in a single worked example.

## Blocker: the Bradley worked example does not fit its own data

**Where:** Appendix A, "Worked Example: Determining $\kappa$ from Bradley's
Observations", and the table labelled `tab:bradley-raw-data`.

The section prints thirteen zenith distances and states that fitting
$z(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi) + B$ to them yields $A \approx 20.5$,
$\phi \approx -1.05$ rad, $B \approx -0.2$, with residuals "typically 1 to 2
arcseconds".

With $\omega$ fixed at $2\pi/365.25$, that model is linear in
$(A\cos\phi, A\sin\phi, B)$, so the least-squares fit is exact and there is
nothing to tune. Fitting the printed table gives:

| Quantity | Book | Fit to the book's own table |
|---|---|---|
| Amplitude $A$ | 20.5 arcsec | 20.54 arcsec |
| Phase $\phi$ | -1.05 rad (-60°) | -0.634 rad (-36.3°) |
| Offset $B$ | -0.2 arcsec | -1.02 arcsec |
| Residual RMS | 1 to 2 arcsec | 3.15 arcsec, largest 8.23 |

The amplitude agrees. Nothing else does. The phase is out by 24 degrees, the
offset by a factor of five, and the residuals are two to three times what the
text claims, with one point off by more than eight arc-seconds. The book uses
those residuals as evidence of "the measurement precision of the zenith
sector", so the number is doing argumentative work and is wrong.

Either the table was generated to illustrate a fit that was written first, or
the fit was quoted from elsewhere and never run against the table. Either way
the section currently shows a reader how to do a calculation and then reports
a different answer than the calculation gives.

**Two further problems in the same section.**

The text says "Bradley selected 23 clear nights of observation." The table
prints thirteen rows. One of those numbers is wrong.

The table's header column reads "Date (1726)", but the Julian day numbers run
from 37.4 to 368.4. Day 368 is past the end of 1726, and the last row is
labelled 1 December. If the series begins in December 1725, as the
surrounding text says, then the day numbers are counted from an epoch the
table never states, and the column heading is misleading.

**Recommended action.** Decide what this example is. If it is a demonstration
of method, say so plainly, generate the data from the known aberration
constant with stated noise, and print the fit that the data actually
produces. If it is meant to be Bradley's observations, the values have to
come from the 1728 paper or from Rigaud's edition of Bradley's
*Miscellaneous Works and Correspondence* (1832), with a citation, and the fit
recomputed from them. What is there now cannot be repaired by adjusting a
number, because the table and the result contradict each other.

## Roemer's speed of light

**Where:** Appendix A, "Implications for the Speed of Light".

The book writes that Bradley's result is "consistent with measurements from
other methods (such as Roemer's determination of $c \approx 2.75 \times 10^5$
km/s from Jupiter's moon eclipses)".

Roemer did not publish a speed. He gave a light-crossing time for a distance,
and every speed attributed to him is that time combined with a value of the
astronomical unit chosen by someone else. Using Roemer's 1676 figure with
Huygens's contemporary estimate gives roughly $2.1$ to $2.2 \times 10^5$
km/s, which is the number usually quoted in the history of the measurement.
$2.75 \times 10^5$ km/s is close to the value obtained with a modern
astronomical unit, which is a different and anachronistic calculation.

**Recommended action.** State whose value of the astronomical unit is being
used, or replace the figure with Roemer's light-time itself, which is what he
actually determined. Either way it needs a citation.
