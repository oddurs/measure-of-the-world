# Appendix G: Primary Source Verification

Fact-check of the six attributed quotations in `src/appendices/appendix-g.tex`.

Every excerpt was checked against scanned primary text, not against secondary
descriptions. Where a source is public domain, the OCR of the original scan was
downloaded and searched directly. The passages quoted below are what those scans
actually say.

## Summary

| # | Excerpt | Verdict |
|---|---------|---------|
| 1 | Flamsteed on systematic observation | FABRICATED |
| 2 | Bradley on stellar aberration | FABRICATED |
| 3 | Maskelyne on the Nautical Almanac | FABRICATED |
| 4 | Airy on systematic error analysis | FABRICATED (cited work does not exist) |
| 5 | 1884 International Meridian Conference resolution | FABRICATED |
| 6 | Dyson, Eddington & Davidson 1919 eclipse report | FABRICATED |

None of the six quotations appears in the work it is attributed to. Four of the
six cited works exist but do not contain the quoted text. One cited work
(excerpt 4) does not exist at all. One cited work (excerpt 1) exists but is in
Latin and is not structured as the book describes.

Two of the six also contain quantitative claims that are factually wrong
independent of the wording: excerpt 5 attributes a time-zone resolution to a
conference that passed no such resolution, and excerpt 6 misstates the
measured deflection values.

Recommended action for the appendix as a whole: **do not print any of the six
excerpts as quotations.** Replace each with either a genuine passage from the
verified source, or with the book's own prose describing what the source says.

## Excerpt 1: John Flamsteed on Systematic Observation

**Verdict: FABRICATED.**

**As printed.** Attributed to "Flamsteed's introduction to *Historia Coelestis
Britannica* (1725)", beginning "The irregular motions of the Heavens require not
only the observations of one or two nights, but the labour of many years, and
the collection of many hands..."

**Correct citation.** John Flamsteed, *Historiae Coelestis Britannicae Volumen
...*, 3 vols., London, 1725. Internet Archive scan of vol. 1:
https://archive.org/details/historia-coelestis-britannica-vol.-1 (vol. 3:
https://archive.org/details/historia-coelestis-britannica-vol-3). The English
translation of the preface is Allan Chapman (ed.), *The Preface to John
Flamsteed's Historia Coelestis Britannica*, translated by Alison Dione Johnson,
National Maritime Museum, Maritime Monographs and Reports No. 52, Greenwich,
1982.

**What the source actually is.** The 1725 work is in Latin throughout. The
Internet Archive catalogue record gives the language as `lat`, and the title page
of the scan reads:

> Flamsteed, John / Ptolemaeus, Claudius / Brahe, Tycho
> Historiae Coelestis Britannicae Volumen ...
> Londoni 1725

The book has no "introduction" in the sense implied. Its long preface is the
*Prolegomena* to the star catalogue, and it stands at the front of **volume 3**,
not volume 1. The full volume title describes it as *"Complectens Praefationem
spatiosam (sive in Stellarum Fixarum Catalogum Prolegomena)"*.

**Difference from what the book prints.** The book presents fluent
eighteenth-century English prose as Flamsteed's own words from a Latin work.
Searches for the quotation's most distinctive phrases, "the labour of many
years, and the collection of many hands" and "that degree of accuracy which the
human senses", return no match anywhere: not in the scanned original, not in any
scholarly or popular source. A genuinely quoted passage from a famous preface
would leave a trace. This one does not.

**Recommended action.** Remove the quotation. If a Flamsteed excerpt is wanted,
draw a real one from the Chapman/Johnson translation of the *Prolegomena*, or
from Francis Baily, *An Account of the Revd John Flamsteed*, London, 1835, which
prints Flamsteed's autobiographical writings in English. Either way the excerpt
must be labelled as a translation, with the translator named.

**Context paragraph.** Substantially correct. Flamsteed was appointed the King's
"astronomical observator" on 4 March 1675 and remained in charge until his death
on 31 December 1719, so "44-year tenure" is right. One nuance: the Greenwich
building was not inaugurated until 1676 and Flamsteed moved in that July, so his
tenure *at Greenwich* is a year shorter than his tenure in the post. Consider
"His 44 years as Astronomer Royal (1675--1719)".

## Excerpt 2: James Bradley on Stellar Aberration

**Verdict: FABRICATED.** The source exists, the printed title is wrong, and the
quoted text is absent from it.

**As printed.** Attributed to "Bradley's *Letter on a New Discovered Motion of
the Fixed Stars* (1728)", beginning "In the year 1725, in December, I observed
with a zenith-sector, placed in the vertical to the star $\gamma$ Draconis, which
passed almost through my Zenith."

**Correct citation.** James Bradley, "IV. A Letter from the Reverend Mr. James
Bradley Savilian Professor of Astronomy at Oxford, and F.R.S. to Dr. Edmond
Halley Astronom. Reg. &c. giving an Account of a new discovered Motion of the
Fix'd Stars", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London*, vol.
35, no. 406 (1727--1728), pp. 637--661. Scanned full text:
https://archive.org/details/philtrans02540261 . Publisher page:
https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/issue/35/406 .

**What the source actually says.** The letter opens, not with a first-person
account of a December 1725 observation, but with an address to Halley:

> SIR,
> YOU having been pleafed to exprefs your Satisfaction with what I had an
> Opportunity fometime ago, of telling you in Converfation, concerning fome
> Obfervations, that were making by our late worthy and ingenious Friend, the
> honourable Samuel Molyneux Efquire, and which have fince been continued and
> repeated by my felf, in order to determine the Parallax of the fixt Stars; I
> fhall now beg leave to lay before you a more particular Account of them.

The December 1725 observations are described several pages later, and the facts
differ materially from the book's version:

> Mr. Molyneux's Apparatus was compleated and fitted for obferving about the End
> of November 1725, and on the third Day of December following, the bright Star
> in the Head of Draco (marked $\gamma$ by Bayer) was for the firft Time
> obferved, as it paffed near the Zenith, and its Situation carefully taken with
> the Inftrument.
>
> ... It was chiefly therefore Curiofity that tempted me (being then at Kew,
> where the Inftrument was fixed) to prepare for obferving the Star on December
> 17th, when having adjufted the Inftrument as ufual, I perceived that it paffed
> a little more Southerly this Day than when it was obferved before.

Bradley's statement of the explanation, which the book paraphrases into its
closing sentence, actually reads:

> At laft I conjectured, that all the Phaenomena hitherto mentioned, proceeded
> from the progreffive Motion of Light and the Earth's annual Motion in its
> Orbit. For I perceived, that, if Light was propagated in Time, the apparent
> Place of a fixt Object would not be the fame when the Eye is at Reft, as when
> it is moving in any other Direction, than that of the Line paffing through the
> Eye and Object.

**Difference from what the book prints.** Four separate errors, any one of which
would be disqualifying.

- **Wording.** No sentence of the printed excerpt appears in the letter.
- **Whose instrument.** The December 1725 observations were made with **Samuel
  Molyneux's** zenith sector at **Kew**, not with Bradley's own instrument.
  Bradley's own zenith sector at Wanstead was not erected until August 1727. The
  book's "I observed with a zenith-sector ... which passed almost through my
  Zenith" transfers Molyneux's instrument and site to Bradley.
- **Sequence of reasoning.** The book has Bradley notice motion, rule out
  parallax, and reach the light-speed conclusion in one smooth paragraph. The
  letter records the opposite: he and Molyneux first suspected observational
  error, then instrumental change, then a nutation of the Earth's axis, then
  the plumb-line, then refraction, and only "at laft" arrived at the progressive
  motion of light.
- **"Corresponded exactly to the annual changes in the Earth's velocity."** The
  displacement tracks the *direction* of the Earth's velocity, not changes in its
  magnitude. This is a physics error introduced by the fabricated text.

**Recommended action.** Replace with one of the two genuine passages quoted
above. Both are short, vivid, and public domain. Preserve the original spelling
or state explicitly that spelling has been modernized. Correct the title to the
real one, or cite it in short form as Bradley's 1728 letter to Halley.

**Context paragraph.** Three problems.

- **"Bradley's 1725 discovery of stellar aberration."** December 1725 is when the
  anomalous motion was first *noticed*. Bradley did not identify it as aberration
  until 1728; the letter was read to the Royal Society in the winter of 1728--29
  and published in the number for 1728. Prefer "Bradley's discovery, begun in
  1725 and explained in 1728".
- **"Determined the speed of light via parallactic displacement."** Wrong
  mechanism, and Bradley's whole point was that the effect is *not* parallax. The
  letter derives a light travel time from the Sun to the Earth "in 8' 12"", and
  notes this falls between Roemer's roughly 11 minutes and the roughly 7 minutes
  others had inferred. Say "via the aberration of starlight", and if a number is
  wanted, use the light-time of 8 min 12 s.
- **"Aberration amplitude of 20.5 arcseconds."** Defensible as the modern
  constant of aberration (20.4955"), but it is not Bradley's figure. Bradley
  measured a total annual range of 40".4 for $\gamma$ Draconis, giving a
  semi-amplitude of 20".2. If the sentence is about what Bradley achieved, use
  20".2.

## Excerpt 3: Nevil Maskelyne on the Nautical Almanac

**Verdict: FABRICATED.**

**As printed.** Attributed to "Maskelyne's preface to the first *Nautical
Almanac* (1767)", beginning "The problem of determining the longitude at sea has
long been recognized as one of the most important challenges of navigation."

**Correct citation.** *The Nautical Almanac and Astronomical Ephemeris, for the
Year 1767*, published by order of the Commissioners of Longitude, London: printed
by W. Richardson and S. Clark, 1766. Preface signed "NEVIL MASKELYNE, Astronomer
Royal". Scanned full text:
https://archive.org/details/the-nautical-almanac-and-astronomical-ep (second
scan: https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_the-nautical-almanac-and_great-britain-commissio_1766).

**What the source actually says.** The preface is short. It is reproduced here in
full, because the contrast with the printed excerpt is the finding.

> THE Commiffioners of Longitude, in purfuance of the Powers vefted in them by a
> late Act of Parliament, prefent the Publick with the NAUTICAL ALMANAC and
> ASTRONOMICAL EPHEMERIS for the Year 1767, to be continued annually; a Work
> which muft greatly contribute to the Improvement of Aftronomy, Geography, and
> Navigation. This Ephemeris contains every Thing effential to general Ufe that
> is to be found in any Ephemeris hitherto publifhed, with many other ufeful and
> interefting Particulars never yet offered to the Publick in any Work of this
> Kind. The Tables of the Moon had been brought by the late Profeffor Mayer of
> Gottingen to a fufficient Exactnefs to determine the Longitude at Sea, within a
> Degree, as appeared by the Trials of feveral Perfons who made Ufe of them. The
> Difficulty and Length of the neceffary Calculations feemed the only Obftacles to
> hinder them from becoming of general Ufe: To remove which this Ephemeris was
> made; the Mariner being hereby relieved from the Neceffity of calculating the
> Moon's Place from the Tables, and afterwards computing the Diftance to Seconds
> by Logarithms, which are the principal and only very delicate Part of the
> Calculus; fo that the finding the Longitude by the Help of the Ephemeris is now
> in a Manner reduced to the Computation of the Time, an Operation equal to that
> of an Azimuth, and the Correction of the Diftance on account of Refraction and
> Parallax, which is alfo rendered very eafy by either of the Two Methods invented
> by Mr. Lyons and Mr. Dunthorne, and publifhed among the Tables requifite to be
> ufed with the Ephemeris.
>
> By Defire of the Commiffioners of Longitude I drew up the Explanation and Ufe of
> the Articles contained in the Ephemeris, and the Inftructions, with Examples,
> for finding the Longitude at Sea by the Help of the fame. ...
>
> All the Articles of the Ephemeris were computed by Two feparate Perfons, and
> examined by a Third, except the Moon's Longitude, Latitude, Right Afcenfion,
> Declination, Semidiameter, and Parallax, which, for Noon, were computed by One
> Perfon, and for Midnight by another, and the Truth of thefe Calculations
> afcertained by means of Differences, which, for the Moon's Longitude, were
> carried as far as the Fourth Order.
>
> NEVIL MASKELYNE, Astronomer Royal.

**Difference from what the book prints.** Nothing in the printed excerpt appears
in the preface. Beyond the wording:

- **Voice.** The opening paragraph is written in the third person on behalf of the
  Commissioners of Longitude. Maskelyne's own first person appears only from the
  second paragraph, and describes what he personally drew up and calculated. The
  book's "I have therefore directed that the Greenwich Observatory's observations
  and calculations be organized and published" attributes an executive act to
  Maskelyne that the preface does not claim.
- **Chronometers.** The preface never mentions marine chronometers. The book's
  "While marine chronometers hold great promise" is invented, and it inserts a
  rivalry the document does not express.
- **Accuracy, and this is the serious one.** The preface states that Mayer's
  lunar tables were accurate enough "to determine the Longitude at Sea, **within a
  Degree**". At the equator one degree of longitude is about 60 nautical miles.
  The book's fabricated text promises longitude "to within a few nautical miles",
  overstating the document's own claim by more than an order of magnitude.
- **"With every lunation."** The Almanac is an annual publication tabulating
  lunar distances at three-hour intervals. The phrase is meaningless as printed.

**Recommended action.** Remove the quotation and replace it with the genuine
preface, which is public domain, brief, and makes the book's point better than
the invention does. The "within a Degree" sentence and the final paragraph on
double computation and checking by a third person are the strongest excerpts.

**Context paragraph.** Mostly sound, with one overstatement. "The first
systematic application of astronomical observations to a practical navigation
problem" is not supportable: lunar and Jovian-satellite methods for longitude
were applied systematically well before 1766, and the Almanac's own preface
positions it as removing the *computational* obstacle to an existing method
rather than as inventing the application. Suggest "the first sustained,
state-funded ephemeris produced specifically for navigators". Also note that the
Almanac *for* 1767 was published in 1766; the appendix's "(1767)" should be
glossed if the publication date matters.

## Excerpt 4: George Airy on Systematic Error Analysis

**Verdict: FABRICATED. The cited work does not exist.**

**As printed.** Attributed to "Airy's *On the Determination of the Differential
Refraction at Different Altitudes* (1842)."

**Correct citation.** There is none, because there is no such paper.

**How this was established.** The Royal Society's *Catalogue of Scientific Papers
(1800--1863)*, vol. 1, lists every paper Airy published in the period, numbered
and in chronological order.
(https://archive.org/details/catalogueofscien01royarich). The entries spanning
1839--1845 are numbers 44 through 59, and they run:

> 44. On the determination of the orbits of comets from observations.
> 45. On the regulator of the clock-work for effecting uniform movement of equatoreals.
> 46. Observations of the total Solar Eclipse of 1842, July 7.
> 47. On the principle of an escapement recently invented ... for the Observatory of Pulkowa.
> 48. A catalogue of 726 stars deduced from the observations made at the Cambridge Observatory.
> 49. On a new apparent Polarity of light.
> 50. (Bakerian Lecture). On the theoretical explanation of an apparent new Polarity in Light.
> 51. On the diffraction of an annular aperture.
> 52. Reply to Prof. Challis on the investigation of the resistance of the air to an oscillating sphere.
> 53. Supplement to a paper "On the theoretical explanation of an apparent new Polarity in Light."
> 54. On a new construction of the going-fusee.
> 55. On the laws of the rise and fall of the tide in the Thames.
> 56. Magnetic Phenomena.
> 57. Observations of the star Groombridge, 1830.
> 58. On the laws of individual tides at Southampton and Ipswich.
> 59. Right ascensions and north polar distances of the comet of Faye.

No paper on refraction appears. Searching Airy's entire entry in the catalogue
for "refract" returns only three hits, all from 1830--1833 and all concerning the
*double* refraction of quartz, which is crystal optics and unrelated. Independent
web searches for the exact printed title return nothing. The Wikisource
bibliography of Airy lists six works for 1842, none of them this.

**Difference from what the book prints.** The excerpt is not a modified
quotation. It is text attributed to a document that was never written.

Two internal markers confirm this independently of the bibliography.

- **"Standard deviation."** The term was coined by Karl Pearson in 1894. It could
  not appear in an 1842 text.
- **"This I term the 'personal equation.'"** Airy neither coined the term nor
  discovered the effect. The phenomenon was identified at Greenwich in 1796, when
  Maskelyne found his assistant David Kinnebrook recording transits consistently
  late, and it was established as a systematic, measurable observer constant by
  F. W. Bessel in 1823. Bessel himself did not use the phrase; it was attached to
  his work by his later editor Rudolf Engelmann. Airy, Astronomer Royal from
  1835, worked with personal equations as an inherited concept.

**Recommended action.** Delete the excerpt and its citation entirely. If the book
needs Airy on error analysis, the real work is George Biddell Airy, *On the
Algebraical and Numerical Theory of Errors of Observations and the Combination of
Observations*, London: Macmillan, 1861
(https://archive.org/details/onalgebraicalnum00airyrich). Substitute a genuine
passage from it. Do not reuse any part of the printed text.

**Context paragraph.** Requires rewriting. "Airy's recognition of systematic
errors and invention of statistical methods to quantify them" overstates his role
and, given the personal-equation history above, misattributes the concept. The
Airy Transit Circle date is correct: the instrument was designed by Airy and its
first observation was taken on 4 January 1851.

## Excerpt 5: The 1884 International Meridian Conference Resolution

**Verdict: FABRICATED.** This is the most clear-cut case in the appendix, because
the conference's resolutions are recorded verbatim and are unambiguous.

**As printed.** Attributed to "the Proceedings of the International Meridian
Conference (1884, Washington, DC)", beginning "RESOLVED: That the following ten
meridians be designated as the basis for the reckoning of longitude..." and
closing with a recommendation for a universal day "with the boundary lines of
time zones following, as closely as possible, meridians at intervals of 15°."

**Correct citation.** *International Conference Held at Washington for the Purpose
of Fixing a Prime Meridian and a Universal Day. October, 1884. Protocols of the
Proceedings*, Washington: Gibson Bros., 1884. Project Gutenberg ebook 17759:
https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/17759 .

**What the source actually says.** The Final Act, adopted unanimously on 22
October 1884, contains **seven** resolutions. They are reproduced here in full,
because the appendix's version bears no relation to them.

> I. "That it is the opinion of this Congress that it is desirable to adopt a
> single prime meridian for all nations, in place of the multiplicity of initial
> meridians which now exist."
>
> II. "That the Conference proposes to the Governments here represented the
> adoption of the meridian passing through the centre of the transit instrument at
> the Observatory of Greenwich as the initial meridian for longitude."
>
> III. "That from this meridian longitude shall be counted in two directions up to
> 180 degrees, east longitude being plus and west longitude minus."
>
> IV. "That the Conference proposes the adoption of a universal day for all
> purposes for which it may be found convenient, and which shall not interfere
> with the use of local or other standard time where desirable."
>
> V. "That this universal day is to be a mean solar day; is to begin for all the
> world at the moment of mean midnight of the initial meridian, coinciding with
> the beginning of the civil day and date of that meridian; and is to be counted
> from zero up to twenty-four hours."
>
> VI. "That the Conference expresses the hope that as soon as may be practicable
> the astronomical and nautical days will be arranged everywhere to begin at mean
> midnight."
>
> VII. "That the Conference expresses the hope that the technical studies designed
> to regulate and extend the application of the decimal system to the division of
> angular space and of time shall be resumed, so as to permit the extension of
> this application to all cases in which it presents real advantages."

Resolution II passed 22 to 1, with San Domingo against and Brazil and France
abstaining. Resolution V passed 15 to 2 with 7 abstentions, and Turkey later
changed its vote to the negative at the 1 November session.

**Difference from what the book prints.**

- **"The following ten meridians."** No resolution says this, and the printed
  excerpt then lists only two items, which are the same meridian and its
  antipode. The sentence is internally incoherent as well as absent from the
  record.
- **The hydrographic sentence.** The strings "geographic work" and "can be made
  immediately" do not occur anywhere in the 441,000-character proceedings.
- **The time-zone paragraph.** This is the most consequential fabrication. The
  conference adopted **no** resolution on time zones. Searching the full
  proceedings for "15 degrees", "fifteen degrees", "time zone" and "time-zone"
  returns four hits, every one of them from a delegate's speech or discussion,
  none from any adopted resolution. Zone time was a matter for individual
  governments and spread over the following decades; the conference confined
  itself to the universal day of Resolution V.
- **"Extending eastward."** Not in Resolution V, which specifies only that the
  universal day begins at mean midnight of the initial meridian and is counted
  from zero to twenty-four hours.

**Recommended action.** Replace the excerpt with Resolutions I through V exactly
as quoted above, which are public domain and directly serve the chapter's
argument. Delete the invented time-zone paragraph without replacement.

**Context paragraph.** Contains a factual error that must be fixed even if the
quotation is replaced. "By adopting Greenwich as the Prime Meridian and defining
24 hourly time zones, the conference created the framework for synchronized
global timekeeping" is wrong on the second half. The conference defined a
universal day, not time zones. Suggested repair: "By adopting Greenwich as the
prime meridian and defining a universal day counted from mean midnight at that
meridian, the conference created the framework on which national time-zone
systems were subsequently built."

## Excerpt 6: Dyson's 1919 Eclipse Expedition Report

**Verdict: FABRICATED.** The source exists and is correctly attributed, the
printed title is incomplete, and the quoted text is absent. The numbers it
reports are wrong.

**As printed.** Attributed to "Dyson, Eddington, and Davidson's *A Determination
of the Deflection of Light by the Sun's Gravitational Field* (1920)", and stating
that the mean displacements "agreed closely with Einstein's prediction of light
deflection: approximately 1.75 arc-seconds for stars at the solar limb, compared
to Einstein's theoretical prediction of 1.745 arc-seconds."

**Correct citation.** F. W. Dyson, A. S. Eddington and C. Davidson, "IX. A
Determination of the Deflection of Light by the Sun's Gravitational Field, from
Observations made at the Total Eclipse of May 29, 1919", *Philosophical
Transactions of the Royal Society of London, Series A*, vol. 220 (1920), pp.
291--333. Received 30 October, read 6 November 1919, published 27 April 1920.
Scanned full text: https://archive.org/details/philtrans06337895 . Publisher page:
https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsta/article/220/571-581/291/40498 .

Note the printed title omits the second half, ", from Observations made at the
Total Eclipse of May 29, 1919", which is part of the actual title.

**What the source actually says.** From Section V, General Conclusions:

> 39. In summarising the results of the two expeditions, the greatest weight must
> be attached to those obtained with the 4-inch lens at Sobral. ... These plates
> gave
>
> From declinations . . . 1"·94
> From right ascensions . . . 2"·06
>
> The result from declinations is about twice the weight of that from right
> ascensions, so that the mean result is
>
> 1"·98
>
> with a probable error of about ±0"·12.
>
> The Principe observations were generally interfered with by cloud. ... The
> deflection obtained was [1"·61]. The probable error is about ±0"·305, so that
> the result has much less weight than the preceding.
>
> Both of these point to the full deflection 1"·75 of Einstein's generalised
> relativity theory, the Sobral results definitely, and the Principe results
> perhaps with some uncertainty. There remain the Sobral astrographic plates which
> gave the deflection
>
> 0"·93
>
> discordant by an amount much beyond the limits of its accidental error. For the
> reasons already described at length not much weight is attached to this
> determination.

And the paper's closing judgement:

> Thus the results of the expeditions to Sobral and Principe can leave little
> doubt that a deflection of light takes place in the neighbourhood of the sun and
> that it is of the amount demanded by Einstein's generalised theory of
> relativity, as attributable to the sun's gravitational field.

**Difference from what the book prints.**

- **Wording.** No sentence of the printed excerpt appears in the paper.
- **The measured values.** The book states the observed displacements were
  "approximately 1.75 arc-seconds". They were not. Three determinations were
  reported: 1"·98 ± 0"·12 (Sobral 4-inch), 1"·61 ± 0"·305 (Príncipe), and 0"·93
  (Sobral astrographic, which the authors set aside as discordant). None is 1.75.
  Printing 1.75 as the measurement erases the entire evidential structure of the
  paper, including the discarded plates that later became the centre of a
  historical controversy about the expedition.
- **Einstein's prediction.** The paper gives it as 1"·75, stated three times. The
  book's "1.745 arc-seconds" appears nowhere in the paper. The book has inverted
  the two numbers: it reports the prediction as the measurement, and invents a
  spuriously precise prediction to sit beside it.
- **The comparison plates.** The book says the eclipse plates were compared with
  "photographic plates taken in the same regions of the sky after the eclipse".
  This is true only for Sobral, where the party stayed on and returned on 9 July
  for comparison plates. At Príncipe the comparison photographs were taken at
  Oxford by F. A. Bellamy **before** the instrument was dismounted and shipped,
  that is, before the eclipse. Eddington also photographed a separate check field
  at both Oxford and Príncipe to control for the difference in observing
  conditions.
- **Alphabetical order of authors.** Not an error, but worth noting for the
  bibliography: the paper's byline order is Dyson, Eddington, Davidson, which the
  book follows correctly.

**Recommended action.** Replace with the General Conclusions passage quoted
above, which is public domain and far more interesting than the invention,
because it shows the authors weighing three discordant results. At minimum, the
numbers 1.75 and 1.745 must be removed from the appendix wherever they appear as
measurements.

**Context paragraph.** One claim needs softening. "The first direct experimental
evidence for Einstein's general relativity" is too strong: general relativity's
first empirical success was the 1915 explanation of the anomalous perihelion
precession of Mercury, a known discrepancy the theory accounted for. The 1919
eclipse was the first confirmation of a *novel* prediction, and the first direct
measurement of gravitational light deflection. Suggested repair: "the first
confirmation of a new prediction of general relativity, and the first direct
measurement of the deflection of light by gravity."

## Method note

Sources were retrieved as raw OCR text and searched locally, rather than read
through summarizing intermediaries, so that the absence of a phrase could be
established as well as its presence. Files retrieved:

- 1884 Meridian Conference proceedings, Project Gutenberg 17759, 441,722 bytes.
- Bradley 1728 letter, Internet Archive `philtrans02540261`, 42,014 bytes.
- Nautical Almanac for 1767, Internet Archive `the-nautical-almanac-and-astronomical-ep`, 272,460 bytes.
- Dyson, Eddington & Davidson 1920, Internet Archive `philtrans06337895`, 130,506 bytes.
- Royal Society *Catalogue of Scientific Papers* vol. 1, Internet Archive `catalogueofscien01royarich`, 5,513,897 bytes.
- Flamsteed, *Historia Coelestis Britannica* vol. 1, Internet Archive `historia-coelestis-britannica-vol.-1`, 1,166,474 bytes.

Long-s characters in eighteenth-century scans are rendered by OCR as `f`. The
quoted passages above preserve the OCR forms, lightly corrected for obvious
scanning artefacts only where the reading is unambiguous.
