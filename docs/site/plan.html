01State of the manuscript

Read the following the way the book itself reads an instrument: as an error budget, where every unverified line is a source of error that has to be driven to zero before publication. The bars show where verification has actually reached, not where it is planned to reach.

What verification found

All six primary-source quotations in Appendix G were fabricated. Each was checked against scanned originals rather than descriptions of them, and not one of the six passages appears in the work it was attributed to. One cited a paper by Airy that was never written, confirmed against the Royal Society's exhaustive catalogue of his output; its invented text used a statistical term coined 52 years after the supposed publication date. The appendix has been rebuilt from verified text.

Appendix C had listed five people as Astronomer Royal who never held the office, including one who appears not to exist. Appendix B had 32 of 34 instrument entries wrong. Appendix H had 32 of 79 chronology entries wrong, including a satellite launched in 1989 credited with a method in 1821.

The pattern is consistent and it sets the rule for everything that follows. The draft's standing editorial instruction was not to independently verify citations. That instruction is what let all of this through. Nothing in this book can be printed on the strength of its already being written down.

02Operating principles

Four rules that decide how every stage below is built.

  • Everything is a file in git. Claims, verification status, figure specifications, image licences, review findings. No state lives only in a chat window or a spreadsheet. One pull request per chapter per stage keeps every change reviewable.
  • Tools find, people decide. Scripts and agents extract claims, look up sources, lint prose, render proofs and flag defects. The author signs off on every factual correction and every figure.
  • Gates, not impressions. A chapter is finished when it passes a fixed list of checks. A single command reports where every chapter stands.
  • Sources over memory. No agent may correct a fact from its own knowledge. It records the claim, the source it found, and the proposed change. The source must be a citable document, and it enters the bibliography.

03The five engines

Each is a directory of tracked files plus the scripts that maintain them, wired into make verify, make lint, make figure-qa, make proof and make status. Continuous integration runs all of them on every pull request.

Reference engine

review/refs

Every entry resolves to a real, locatable work, and every citation supports the sentence it is attached to.

  • Resolve. Query Crossref for DOIs, NASA ADS for the historical astronomy journals the book leans on (Philosophical Transactions, Monthly Notices, Astronomische Nachrichten), and Open Library plus Google Books for monographs. Each key lands as resolved, ambiguous, or not found.
  • Enrich. Attach a DOI, ADS bibcode, ISBN and stable URL so a reader can actually reach the source.
  • Lint. Required fields per entry type, consistent name forms, no duplicate works under two keys, and page numbers on every citation that supports a specific figure or quotation.
  • Link. Each verified claim names the key and page that supports it. A citation supporting nothing gets removed.
  • Fill gaps. Standard works the book should be drawing on where it currently cites nothing: Howse on Greenwich time, the three-volume Greenwich Observatory, Chapman on circular division, Baily on Flamsteed, Betts on chronometers.

Fact-check engine

review/claims

A ledger of every checkable statement, each marked verified, corrected, or removed, with its source recorded.

  • Extract. Split each chapter into sentences and keep those carrying a date, a number with a unit, a proper name, a quotation, or a superlative. Expect 150 to 250 per chapter, written to a YAML ledger with an identifier, the text, a type, a status and a source field.
  • Check in risk order. Appendix G first, because its primary-source excerpts must match the originals word for word and carry the highest fabrication risk. Then appendices C, H and B for people, tenures and instrument specifications. Then the numbers inside derivations. Then the narrative chapters.
  • Recompute the mathematics. Every derivation and worked example gets a short script that reproduces its result numerically: the aberration constant, the refraction table, the lunar-distance clearing example, the equation of time, pendulum temperature error, the chronometer trial rates. A mismatch between script and text is a defect.
  • Gate. No chapter leaves this stage with an unverified claim. Anything that cannot be sourced is rewritten or cut. Encyclopaedias are a lead, never a source.

Editorial engine

review/critique

Prose that holds a consistent voice across 87,000 words written in bursts.

  • Mechanical lint. Rules drawn from the existing style guide: no contractions, no abbreviating right ascension, sentences over 35 words, passive density, forward references without local context, jargon undefined on first use, repeated sentence openers.
  • Structural critique. One agent pass per chapter against a fixed rubric. Does the opening scene earn its place, does every section advance the argument, where does the mathematics arrive unmotivated, where does the text assert precision it does not demonstrate, what is missing, what should be cut. Findings numbered so each can be accepted or rejected.
  • Consistency sweep. One term per concept, one spelling per name, one format for units and dates, one way of naming each instrument, across all 34 files.
  • Human copyedit. Budget for a professional. No tool substitutes for one on a 456-page book.

Visual engine

figures/

Every figure earns its place, reads at printed size, and carries a clear licence. Detailed in section 04.

  • A written specification per figure, a shared design system, automated quality checks, and a manifest that makes an unlicensed image a build failure.

Proof engine

review/layout

A file the printer accepts and a book that reads correctly on paper.

  • Render and report. Every page to an image; overfull lines mapped back to source file and line; floats stranded far from their reference; widows, orphans and stray blank pages; figures scaled below half or above their natural size.
  • Printer checks. Fonts embedded, page boxes correct, image resolution at printed size, and a PDF/X conversion for the Mixam upload.
  • Spreads. Facing pages reviewed as pairs, since that is how the book is actually read.
  • Index. Candidate entries proposed per chapter from names, instruments and concepts, then approved by hand. Target 600 to 900 with cross references.

04Redoing the visuals

All 116 figures were drafted in one session and none had a stated purpose. Legibility is now fixed and every figure has a written specification. What follows is the redraw itself, which is the part that needs the colour decision settled first.

Specify before drawing

Every figure gets a short written specification: the question it answers, the data or geometry it shows, where that came from, its form, and the sentence in the text it supports. A figure that answers no question is cut. The count should fall.

Then split by kind

KindCountToolWhy
Data plots~40matplotlib, restyledReal numbers, reproducible from code, already close to right
Instrument schematics~45TikZCurrently drawn with plotting primitives; vector art in the book's own typeface will read far better
Timelines and flowcharts~20TikZ templatesHighly repetitive, so one good template fixes twenty figures at once

A design system, written down

One page defining the palette, line weights, minimum label size at printed width, arrow style, axis conventions and caption rules, implemented in shared code so every new figure is on-system by default. Minimum readable label size is 7 points at final printed width, which most current figures fail once reduced to the 4.5 inch text block.

Checks that run automatically

  • Text bounding boxes tested against each other, which found 96 collisions across 54 figures, and against the shapes that contain them, which found a further 18 labels spilling outside their own circles.
  • Effective font size computed at the width each figure is actually placed at in its chapter, not the width it was drawn at.
  • Bounding box sanity, catching the runaway figure that rendered 5,000 inches wide.
  • 300 dots per inch at printed size, and a greyscale contrast check regardless of which interior is chosen.

Photographs and historical images

The book wants 40 to 60 images and has six. The gap list runs to portraits of every Astronomer Royal through Woolley, Flamsteed House and the Octagon Room, the mural arc, Bradley's zenith sector, Harrison's five timekeepers, the Airy transit circle, the Great Equatorial, the time ball, the 1884 conference, the Herstmonceux domes, sextants and octants, and title pages from the Nautical Almanac and the Historia Coelestis.

Licensing decides where they come from. The book is published under a licence permitting commercial reuse, so Wikimedia Commons public-domain scans, Wellcome Collection, the Library of Congress, and Internet Archive scans of pre-1900 books all work. Royal Museums Greenwich, the Science Museum Group and the Royal Society picture library do not, without a paid licence.

No image enters the book without a manifest entry recording file, source, creator, date, licence, credit line and pixel size. The credits page is generated from that manifest, and an image without one fails the build.

07Open decisions

Five calls only you can make. The first two block the visual engine, so they are worth settling this week.

Colour or greyscale interior

At 456 pages, colour printing costs several times what black and white does. Most of these figures are line diagrams that lose nothing in grey.

SuggestDesign for greyscale with a single accent, then ship colour in the free download and greyscale in the printed interior.

Image licensing budget

The richest Greenwich collections sit behind non-commercial licences that conflict with the book's own licence.

SuggestSet a figure now. If it is zero, the wish list is built only from freely reusable sources, and two of the six existing photographs get replaced.

Who redraws the schematics

Forty-five instrument diagrams is real illustration work. Code keeps them reviewable and in the book's typeface; an illustrator would be better and slower.

SuggestCode, working to written specifications, for a single-author project.

Copyeditor

Eighty-seven thousand words of technical prose written across many sessions will not hold a consistent voice without one.

SuggestHire for phase 3, after the facts are settled and before typesetting locks.

Fate of appendices D and G

The visitor guide dates quickly and the primary-source excerpts are the most expensive material in the book to verify, since each quotation must be matched against an original.

SuggestKeep G and verify it first. Reconsider D, or reduce it to a page of pointers.
